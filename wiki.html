A three-star kitchen, built like a factory twin

This site is a digital twin of The French Laundry's kitchen in Yountville, California, running a full dinner service. It is modelled the way a manufacturing plant is: covers booked are the build plan, every recipe is a bill of materials with a routing, stations are work centers, and a discrete-event simulation drives the brigade, the machines and the stock you see in 3D. Everything runs in your browser from a static GitHub Pages site.

Architecture

The system is a pipeline with one source of truth. Sourced JSON in /data is turned into a production plan and into the simulation's input; the simulation owns the clock; the 3D scene, the dashboard and Studio only read its state and send it commands.

The frame loop

The simulation owns time. Each animation frame advances it by the wall-clock delta times the chosen speed, then the scene and the dashboard mirror the new state. Because every view reads the same state, the 3D cooks, the Gantt chart and the HUD never disagree.

The research story

Nobody handed over blueprints. The French Laundry does not publish its floor plan, its brigade or its prep lists. Everything in this twin was pieced together from what the restaurant, its architects, the trade press, its cookbook and its diners have put in public, and each fact is tagged with where it came from.

Why this kitchen

The brief was a three-star kitchen, as realistic as possible. That made the choice a research question: which three-star kitchen has the most published detail? The French Laundry won clearly. Its 2014 to 2017 rebuild was covered by the architects (Snøhetta), by Architectural Record, and by Foodservice Equipment & Supplies, whose article lists the full equipment schedule down to the make of the slicer. Thomas Keller's 1999 cookbook gives exact quantities for the signature dishes. And diners have written course-by-course accounts of the tasting menu, with timings.

From an article to a moving kitchen

  1. Read and extract. Each source was read for facts the model could use: a dimension, an appliance, a quantity, a time. Each fact became a field in a JSON file with a src list naming where it came from.
  2. Translate into factory terms. Recipes became bills of materials, method steps became routings with a station, appliance, cook and time, and the brigade became a labor pool with skills. See Data and sources.
  3. Fill the gaps honestly. Where nothing was published (portions for dishes without a recipe, most operation times, positions on the floor, ingredient prices), a typical professional value went in, tagged est.
  4. Check it. A validator checks every cross-reference, and a test checks that planning four servings of Oysters and Pearls reproduces the cookbook's quantities.
  5. Run it. The planner explodes covers into grams and minutes, the simulation plays the night, and the 3D scene draws what the simulation is doing.

The research, the data model and the planner were built by one Claude session in the first hours of the project, and handed to the 3D, simulation and Studio sessions as a shared contract of ids. See How it was built.

Counted live from /data. A record is "sourced" when it cites a publication, "estimated" when its only tag is est; many cite a publication for some fields and estimate others.

Production planning (MRP)

src/model/mrp.js runs a classic material requirements planning explosion, the same code in the browser drawer and in npm run plan.

  • Option shares (beef or veal tongue) and supplement uptake (caviar, foie gras) are applied as expected values and rounded up.
  • Sub-assemblies are made in whole batches, so 31 lobsters' worth of demand blanches two batches of 20.
  • Raw materials are grossed up by yieldPct: 900 g of shelled corn needs 2 kg on the cob.
  • Each routing step becomes labor minutes for a role, scaled by laborFraction because a cook is not hands-on while the combi runs, and machine minutes for a piece of equipment.

At 62 covers every station has headroom; at 120 the fish commis needs more prep time than the shift allows. Those are the constraints the simulation then shows live.

Simulation engine

src/sim/engine.js is a discrete-event simulation of the service as a job shop. Time jumps from event to event on a binary heap; between events nothing is computed, which is why a full night of prep and service simulates in about 0.1 seconds.

What flows through it

  1. Reservations [demand]: covers split into parties of one to six, arriving on the chosen seating curve, with no-shows and a finite set of tables. A party that waits longer than its patience walks away.
  2. Prep [pre-production]: the menu is exploded into prep batches with a safety factor. Prep tasks consume raw stock and produce components that service tasks then consume. Items whose lead time cannot fit the day (veal stock, corned tongue, foie torchon) are carry-over stock from a previous day.
  3. Tickets [work orders]: one per table per course. Expo fires each ticket early by the planned lead time of its slowest dish, and staggers the other dishes so they all finish together (just-in-time offset scheduling).
  4. Tasks [routed operations]: each dish is a chain of steps with a station, an appliance type, a role, a duration and a hands-on time, so a combi stays busy after the cook walks away.
  5. Quality gate [final inspection]: QC rejects and plates held too long at the pass are re-fired; waste and food cost are tracked.
  6. Guests [a second production line]: parties are seated, eat each course at their own pace, and leave; every stage has a queue and a KPI.

Dispatching

Whenever something changes, ready tasks are sorted by class (re-fires first, then service, then prep, then pot wash) and by due date, with prep ordered by latest start. Each task then needs, in order: its inputs in stock (or still being prepped, in which case it waits), a free position at its station, a free slot on an appliance of the right type (the least-loaded unit wins), and a cook with that role or skill. Whatever blocks a task is logged, and the blocker with the most waiting in the last half hour is named on the HUD as the constraint, in theory-of-constraints terms.

Randomness and determinism

Operation times are means with lognormal spread (more for hands-on work, less for machine cycles), and appliances break down on an MTBF/MTTR model. All randomness comes from three seeded mulberry32 streams, so the same covers, seating and seed replay the same night to the second. Seeking backwards rebuilds the engine and replays from the seed; that is what makes share links exact.

KPIs

Ticket time p50, p90 and p95 per course; guest wait and course gap against target; throughput and work in process; utilization of cooks, stations and appliances on a 15-minute rolling window; OEE (availability × performance × quality) per appliance; first-pass yield; stockouts, substitutions and comps; food cost, waste and energy; and the bottleneck read-out.

Scenarios and optimizer

Every what-if is a config patch applied to the same seed, so a comparison isolates the change. src/sim/scenarios.js runs a whole service headlessly with runHeadless(), compares two runs on every KPI with compare(), sweeps covers from 20 to 200 to find rated capacity, and writes the shift report as CSV.

The optimizer

A tasting menu has a target length (195 minutes here). When guests sit longer than that, the overrun is the twin's equivalent of missed takt time. src/sim/optimize.js searches two levers that shorten it:

  • An extra cook, cloned from one of the brigade with the same roles, station and shift (one candidate per line role). In the engine this is cfg.extraStaff: [{ like: staffId }].
  • A re-sequenced fire: expo calls each course earlier, by a larger share of its slowest dish's lead time (fireLead, 1× to 1.5×, today 0.9×) plus a longer fixed buffer (leadSec, 75 to 225 s), so plates reach the pass as guests are ready.

Every candidate re-runs the whole night on five seeds and is ranked on the average, so one lucky night cannot win. The two best cooks and the two best fire settings are then combined, since the levers are independent and often stack. Guards flag any plan that wins on meal time by hurting something else: first-pass yield falling more than a point, ticket p90 or table-wait p90 rising more than two minutes, or more guests walking away. The recommendation is the least invasive plan that passes the guards and meets the target: re-sequencing before extra labor, smaller changes first.

Precomputed scenarios

Optimizing one night is a few hundred full-service runs, so the site ships the answers. npm run precompute (scripts/precompute.mjs) optimizes 40, 62, 90 and 120 guests and writes src/sim/precomputed.data.js: each night's KPIs, every candidate ranked, and the recommended plan. src/sim/precomputed.js keys it by a hash of the simulation input, so if /data or the engine changes the site falls back to live runs, and tests/optimizer.test.mjs fails until the file is regenerated. The same file holds each precomputed night's end time, so createSim({ cache }) skips the forecast run when one of them is loaded.

3D scene

src/scene/ builds a photoreal model of the 2017 kitchen (the curved skylit vault, terrazzo floor, the fritted ribbon window onto the garden), the support annex, the culinary garden and a glimpse of the dining room. There are no downloaded models or textures: geometry is generated from /data at load, and every material's texture maps are painted on canvases at runtime from a seeded generator, so they look identical on every load.

Quality modes

Every level shows the same picture: the same materials, in-kitchen reflections, filmic tone mapping, glow on flames and lamps, and soft shadows from the evening sun and the vault lights. Higher levels add sharpness and depth, not a different look.

Auto

Auto reads the GPU name the browser reports and the device memory. Software renderers, coarse-pointer devices and older Intel graphics start on Low; Apple Pro, Max and Ultra chips and discrete cards start on High; everything else starts on Medium. While the view is in use, if frames stay under about 28 fps for three seconds, Auto steps down one level and says so in a caption. It never picks Pro Max and never overrides a level you chose. Your choice is remembered in local storage.

How Pro Max works

While the camera rests, each frame is rendered with a sub-pixel camera offset and with the sun and key light nudged across a disc, following Halton sequences. An accumulation pass blends each frame into a half-float history buffer with a weight of 1/(n+1), so after about 96 frames the image converges to a supersampled still with soft area shadows, like an offline render. Any camera move resets the history.

Example: ?covers=120&seating=rush&t=1.5 opens a rush seating for 120 covers an hour and a half after the doors open, and ?covers=120&cook=ST-SOUS-CHEF-1&fire=1.5&lead=225 opens 120 covers on the optimizer's plan. A re-plan from the dashboard keeps the address bar in sync. Studio encodes its whole setup (dining room, menu, season, look) as base64 JSON in the hash, #s=…, so "62 seats, winter à la carte, candlelit" is a link.

Performance

  • No asset downloads. Geometry and textures are generated at load, so the whole site is a few hundred kilobytes of compressed JavaScript and CSS.
  • Fewer draw calls. Static meshes are merged per material and the brigade's limbs are batched, so the kitchen renders in a small number of draw calls.
  • Event-driven simulation. The engine only computes when something happens, and the scene re-reads it at 6 Hz while tweening in between.
  • Adaptive quality. Auto starts from the GPU and steps down if the frame rate stays low; Pro Max only accumulates while the camera rests.
  • Precomputed answers. The optimizer's results for four guest counts ship with the site, and cached nights skip their forecast run.
  • Measured in the page. src/perf.js times the first 3D frame and the live frame rate; the menu's View section shows them, and npm run perf records them at each quality level on a laptop and a phone viewport. Results and method: docs/performance.md.
  • Headless re-runs. Studio, what-ifs and the capacity sweep re-run the full night without rendering, yielding to the UI between runs.

How it was built

The twin was built by Claude: several sessions working in parallel in one project, each in its own thread, each owning one part of the repository and merging its own pull requests once CI was green. A person set the goal and the bar and steered the work.

Limits and estimates

  • The menu is a composite of documented courses; the real Chef's Tasting Menu changes daily.
  • Portions for courses without a published recipe, operation times not stated in a recipe, brigade size and shifts, equipment positions and slot counts, ingredient prices and eating times are estimates, marked est in the data.
  • The layout follows the published concept and dimensions but is a reconstruction, not a survey.
  • Labor load counts routed operations only; tasting, cleaning down, family meal and the chef's walkthrough are not modelled, so real utilization is higher.
  • Dispatching uses simple priority rules. The optimizer searches staffing and fire-timing levers over whole nights; it does not solve for an optimal schedule of every operation.
